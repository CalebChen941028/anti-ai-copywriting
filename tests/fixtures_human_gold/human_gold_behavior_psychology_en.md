# Empirical Evidence and Stimulus Control in Experimental Analysis of Behavior

**Citation:** Sidman, M. (1960). *Tactics of Scientific Research: Evaluating Experimental Data in Psychology*. Basic Books; Boring, E. G. (1954). *The Nature and History of Experimental Method*.

Although these anecdotes are amusing several hundred years later for their extreme and cumbersome methods of testing, the practicing scientist of today still on occasion faces opposition to conclusions from experiments. Thus, behavior analysts sometimes find themselves in a position in which it is difficult to convince psychologists with different training that changes in contingencies of reinforcement can bring about enduring behavior changes.

For example, I once presented to colleagues in different areas of psychology some empirical data that demonstrated very reliable and precise stimulus control of behavior in rats.
A colleague objected.
He argued that such mechanistic, on–off control of behavior is not psychology. Others in the audience were suspicious of the extremely low variability in the data, which was believed to have come from an equipment error and not from the method of controlling behavior.

The method of comparing two or more series of readings with the same measurement instrument under two or more different conditions is the hallmark of the scientific method (Boring, 1954). Whether the research design is a between-groups or a within-subject comparison of the effects of a manipulation of a variable on behavior, the shared element is always the comparisons of different conditions using the same measurement instrument. In single-case research, the individual serves as his or her own control, and repeated measures across baseline and intervention phases allow researchers to witness dynamic behavioral transitions firsthand.

When investigators rely exclusively on traditional inferential group statistics, the underlying variability of the phenomenon is often obscured by aggregated sample means. An overall curve calculated across forty subjects may smooth out sharp discontinuities, creating the illusion of gradual acquisition when each individual organism actually experienced an abrupt, all-or-none transition. By contrast, experimental analysis of behavior prioritizes the direct manipulation of environmental variables over indirect statistical adjustments.

If the primary objective of a natural science is the prediction and control of functional relations, direct steady-state demonstration across repeated experimental reversals provides far more compelling empirical proof than aggregate significance levels alone. Direct observation of individual trajectories retains the granular physical reality that statistical averaging inevitably discards. Laboratory evidence must ultimately rest upon reproducible functional control rather than formal mathematical post-processing.
